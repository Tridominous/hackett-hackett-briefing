2

Credibility Gap

A trust issue rather than a marketing one.

Corporate clients, family offices and banks check suppliers before buying. They look at Companies House, read the website, and search the company name. That check takes about ten minutes.

At present it finds a store showing sample products and legal pages that were never completed. Whoever is checking draws their own conclusion, and the business rarely hears about it.

The aim is not to appear larger. It is to present the business as accurately as it performs. Seventeen years of trading, genuine reviews, a licensed security operation and nineteen delivery partners is a strong record, and all of it can be verified.

Note

Being checked and found short is worse than being modest and verified. Marketing spend before this is corrected sends people to pages that argue against the business.

Referrals and Digital Approach

The most important section. It addresses a fair concern with a specific plan.

Around 85% of work is reported to arrive by personal recommendation. That is the strongest feature of the business, and nothing in this plan alters it.

The concern, and why it holds

Clients at this level are not looking for a website with a booking button. Some cannot have their movements recorded in an ordinary booking system. That is a security matter rather than a preference. A public booking funnel for private clients should stay off the table.

What still needs attention

Referrals bring clients in. Records keep them. What brings a client back is being remembered: the preferred car, the usual airport, the assistant who books, the detail that went wrong once and was corrected.

At present that information is held largely from memory. It holds until two demanding jobs coincide. Once a client has to explain themselves again, the premium is lost.

Key question

A client who last travelled fourteen months ago calls this evening. What does the person answering the phone know about them, and what happens if that person is unavailable?

Change the systems, not the client experience

Different clients, different routes in

ClientRoute inNot offered
Private and protectedIntroduction, direct line, WhatsApp, a named contactAny public booking form, account or login
Returning private clientsThe same, except their preferences are already on record before they speakMarketing emails, or anything resembling an app
Corporate, embassy, productionAn account with booking, reporting and one monthly invoiceNothing. These buyers want more system, not less, and often will not sign without it
Weddings and eventsAn enquiry, then a person, then a shared planning page once bookedInstant online pricing on a job worth tens of thousands

Strengthening the referral channel

  • Record the introducer on every job. One field.
  • Thank the introducer the same week, and confirm afterwards that the job went well. People refer again once they know their name was safe.
  • Keep a list of twenty contacts worth staying close to: those already sending work, and those who could.
  • Once tracked, the referral share becomes a figure that can be stated in writing. For a firm of this type that is a strong claim to be able to evidence.

Technology Stack

Three separate systems, not one. Treating them as one platform is what makes projects of this kind fail.

1. The website

What people see when they check the company out. Content and design work, updated often.

2. The records

Who the clients are and what they prefer. Private, sensitive, never client facing.

3. Operations

Bookings, drivers, partner allocation, invoices. Live, multi party, transactional.

Industry note

Winning the job and passing delivery to trusted partners is how the wider chauffeur industry operates. The industry term is farming out, and software has existed for it for years. The hardest single requirement here is available to rent rather than build.

In house development capacity

A full stack developer is available to the business, working in Next.js, React, Node and MongoDB, deploying to Vercel or Railway. That changes which parts are worth building rather than renting, and it changes them in a specific direction.

Rented operational software is generally strong on function and weak on appearance. It tends to look the same for every operator. For a business whose proposition is polish and discretion, that matters. The practical answer is to rent the engines and build the surfaces: keep the domain heavy machinery as a subscription, and put a Hackett & Hackett interface over it through its API.

Where the developer time goes first

Order matters more than speed. Content fixes on the existing site come first because they need no code. Then the website rebuild, then the quote form and integrations, then the corporate portal, which is the largest single piece. The internal dashboard follows once there is data worth displaying.

Data boundary

An important design rule. Records for private and protected clients stay inside the rented system, which carries security certifications and a data processing agreement. The custom built portal handles corporate account data only: companies, cost centres, bookings and invoices. That keeps the most sensitive information inside audited software and limits what a bespoke application is responsible for.

Alongside that: not every member of staff sees every client, sensitive clients get restricted records, only necessary data is held, retention periods are agreed, and every supplier signs a data agreement.

Maintenance

Anything custom built needs someone to look after it. Four things reduce that risk: a content management system so routine changes need no developer, standard and widely used tooling, written handover documentation, and automatic deployment so releases do not depend on one person's machine.

WhatsApp

Clients already message on WhatsApp and prefer it. The business version connects those messages to the client records. The client sees no difference. On the company side, whoever picks up the message can see the preferred car and the last journey. Replies to a customer message carry no charge, so the running cost at this volume is small.

Indicative cost

  • Roughly £200 to £600 a month for the rented software, covering records, inbox, messaging, dispatch, accounts and hosting.
  • Custom built parts carry developer time rather than licence fees.
  • Figures are estimates for planning, to be confirmed on quotation.

Not recommended for custom build

  • A platform with a module for every service. Comparable projects run to £60,000 or more and take a year or longer.
  • Dispatch and partner allocation. Years of industry detail sit behind it.
  • Driver tracking, payment handling, and a client database written from scratch.

Performance Measures

Six lines, reviewed weekly. Without them the business is working on impressions.

  • Cash. Bank balance, thirteen week forecast, outstanding invoices.
  • Work coming in. Enquiries, quotes sent, conversion rate, average job value.
  • Work delivered. Jobs completed, on time arrivals, complaints. Nothing left unresolved beyond two days.
  • Referrals. Share of the month arriving by introduction, who sent it, whether they were thanked.
  • Reputation. New reviews, current rating, response time to enquiries.
  • Partners. Work received from partners, and fees earned on work referred out.
Twelve month targets

Proposed targets rather than forecasts. Accounts suitable to submit alongside a corporate tender. More than half of transport income coming from repeat or contracted work. Every client held on record, so that delivery does not depend on one individual being available.

Market Evidence

Each recommendation here rests on published figures rather than opinion.

Market size

  • UK events industry worth £68.7bn in 2025, up from £61.6bn.
  • Corporate work is 55 to 60% of all chauffeur demand, and it renews.
  • London weddings average £27,000, with a quarter of couples spending above £26,000.
  • Household staff placements typically earn agencies 15 to 20% of first year salary.

Competitor outcomes

  • Addison Lee concentrated on London corporate transport and sold for £269m.
  • Blacklane owns no cars. It runs a partner network across 50 countries and is profitable.
  • Both grew by doing fewer things properly rather than adding services.
  • The UK is losing a record number of wealthy residents, so corporate and visiting clients carry more weight each year.
