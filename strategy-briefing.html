Trust and Reputation

Worth being methodical about, because word of mouth carries in both directions.

Referral is the strongest asset in this business. The same network that delivers most of the work also carries any poor account of it. That makes reputation an operating priority rather than a marketing one.

Trust tends to return through a steady supply of recent, checkable evidence rather than through any single statement.

Suggested approach

  • Ask for a review after every completed job. Recent volume matters more than any single older entry.
  • Reply to every review, calmly and factually, including older negative ones. A measured reply usually reads better than silence.
  • Publish the vetting standard. Showing how people are screened today answers the question directly.
  • Name one owner per job on the confirmation, so the client knows who is accountable.
  • Publish written service standards a client can hold the business to.
  • Add third party proof. Security accreditation and industry awards are checkable by strangers in a way that self description is not.
  • Collect case studies with named clients willing to vouch.

Reducing the chance of a repeat

Three controls do most of the work: written vetting for every person who touches a client, one named owner per job, and conduct terms inside every partner and driver agreement.

Note

Publishing current evidence generally works better than responding to older accounts directly. The aim is for the recent record to be the first thing anyone finds.

How Work Arrives

Referral, WhatsApp, Facebook, phone and email. Every one of these stays exactly as it is.

Around 85% of work is reported to arrive by personal recommendation, and the rest comes through Facebook, WhatsApp, the phone and email. Those channels convert because a person answers them. Nothing suggested here changes how a client gets in touch.

The concern, and why it holds

Clients at this level are not looking for a website with a booking button. Some cannot have their movements recorded in an ordinary booking system. That is a security matter rather than a preference. A public booking funnel for private clients is not recommended, now or later.

What is worth attention

Referrals bring clients in. Records keep them. What brings a client back is being remembered: the preferred car, the usual airport, the assistant who books, the detail that went wrong once and was corrected.

At present that sits in memory. It holds until two demanding jobs coincide. Once a client has to explain themselves again, the premium tends to go with it.

Key question

A client who last travelled fourteen months ago calls this evening. What does the person answering the phone know about them, and what happens if that person is unavailable?

Every channel into one place

Step one

However the client prefers

  • Personal introduction
  • WhatsApp
  • Facebook message
  • Phone call
  • Email

Unchanged. No forms, no logins, no accounts.

Step two

One shared place to receive it

  • Every message visible to the whole team
  • Assigned to one named owner
  • Logged against the client record
  • Introducer recorded
  • Nothing sitting in a personal inbox

The only new part, and the client never sees it.

Step three

Routed and delivered

  • Sent to Move, Make or Serve
  • Answered by someone who already knows the client
  • Priced from an agreed rate card
  • Invoiced correctly first time
  • Review requested afterwards

Feeds sections 2 and 13.

Strengthening the referral channel

  • Record the introducer on every job. One field.
  • Thank the introducer the same week, and confirm afterwards that the job went well. People refer again once they know their name was safe.
  • Keep a list of twenty contacts worth staying close to: those already sending work, and those who could.
  • Once tracked, the referral share becomes a figure that can be stated in writing. For a firm of this type that is a strong claim to be able to evidence.

Moving the Website

The search traffic is real and it sits on the older site. Moving carelessly risks losing it.

74pages on the older site, mostly targeting chauffeur searches by location
24blog articles earning traffic on chauffeur and wedding topics
9xheavier: the newer site is around 1.1MB per page against 119KB

The older site is built for search. Its home page is titled for chauffeur hire in London, and it carries separate pages for central, north, south, east and west London, for Croydon, Bromley, Barnet, Enfield and Romford, and for Paris, Dublin, Edinburgh, Hong Kong, Shanghai, Dubai, New York and Washington. There is also a published airport rates page.

The newer site has none of that. Its home page is titled simply Home, which targets nothing. Switching the older site off without carrying its pages across would risk removing the search channel entirely.

Which address to keep

The shorter address looks like the stronger candidate. It holds the search history, it is easier to say on the phone and print on a card, and it makes a shorter email address. The longer one can redirect to it and keep working.

A safe way to move

  1. List every old page before anything changes.All 98 addresses, ordered by the traffic each one brings.
  2. Rebuild the pages that earn traffic.Location pages, the airport rates page and the blog articles. Same subjects, better presentation.
  3. Redirect each old address to its matching new one.One to one, permanent redirects. Pointing everything at the home page loses most of the value.
  4. Keep the page titles that already work.Titles naming the service and the place are why those pages rank.
  5. Ask the nineteen partners to update their links.Those links carry weight and most will update on request.
  6. Submit the new sitemap and watch it for ninety days.Recovery normally takes several weeks. Redirects are best left in place for at least a year.
  7. Reduce the page weight while rebuilding.The newer site is far heavier than the old one, which slows it on phones and works against search ranking.
Note

Google Business Profile, the Facebook page and the reviews sit outside the website and are not affected by the move, provided the address they point to is updated on the day.

Technology Stack

Three separate systems, not one. Treating them as a single platform is what tends to make projects of this kind fail.

1. The website

What people see when they check the business out. Content and search work, updated often.

2. The records

Who the clients are and what they prefer. Private, sensitive, never client facing.

3. Operations

Bookings, drivers, partner allocation, invoices. Live, multi party, transactional.

Industry note

Winning the job and passing delivery to trusted partners is how the wider chauffeur industry operates. The industry calls it farming out, and software has existed for it for years. The hardest single requirement here can be rented rather than built.

Rent the engines, build the surfaces

Rented operational software is strong on function and weak on appearance. It looks the same for every operator. For a business whose proposition is polish and discretion, that matters. The suggested approach is to keep the heavy machinery as a subscription and put a Hackett & Hackett interface over it.

Where Wix fits

Wix handles the website side perfectly well and the team can edit it directly. It is not suited to client records, driver allocation, partner availability or corporate invoicing, which is why those sit in rented software instead.

Data boundary

Records for private and protected clients are best kept inside the rented system, which carries security certifications and a data processing agreement. A custom built portal can then handle corporate account data only: companies, cost centres, bookings and invoices. That keeps the most sensitive information inside audited software.

Alongside that: not every team member sees every client, sensitive clients get restricted records, only necessary data is held, retention periods are agreed, and every supplier signs a data agreement.

Indicative cost

  • Roughly £200 to £600 a month for the rented software, covering records, inbox, messaging, dispatch, accounts and hosting.
  • Custom built parts carry development time rather than licence fees.
  • Figures are estimates for planning, to be confirmed on quotation.

Better not custom built

  • A platform with a module for every service. Comparable projects run to £60,000 or more and take a year or longer.
  • Dispatch and partner allocation. Years of industry detail sit behind it.
  • Driver tracking, payment handling, and a client database written from scratch.

Email and Domain

Small, cheap, and it does more for credibility per pound than almost anything else here.

Email is the first thing a corporate client checks without realising they are checking it. A quote from a personal address reads differently from the same quote sent from the company domain, and quotes that land in a spam folder are simply never answered.

Four things worth putting in place

  • Every client facing address on the company domain. No personal accounts anywhere a client can see.
  • Shared addresses owned by the business, such as bookings and accounts, rather than tied to one person. Nothing is then lost when somebody moves on, and cover during holidays becomes straightforward.
  • Authentication switched on. SPF, DKIM and DMARC are three settings that tell the receiving server the mail is genuinely from Hackett & Hackett. Without them, quotes and invoices are far more likely to land in spam.
  • The short domain for addresses, following the section 8 decision. Shorter is easier to read out on a call and fits on a card.

Avoiding a lock in

The domain matters more than the provider. As long as Hackett & Hackett owns the domain and the provider supports the standard protocols, mail can move to a different provider later without losing an address or a message. All four options above meet that test, so this is a reversible decision rather than a permanent one.

Development Scope

What an in house developer covers, and what that removes from the budget.

A full stack developer is available to the business, working in modern web tooling and deploying to managed hosting. That changes which parts of section 9 are worth building rather than renting, and it means changes can be made in the same week rather than raised as a change request with an outside supplier.

Delivered in house

  • The website move. Page inventory, rebuild, redirect map and monitoring, as set out in section 8.
  • The rebuilt website, with a content management system so the team edits content without needing a developer.
  • The branded quote and enquiry form, feeding the client records.
  • The corporate client portal, the largest single piece.
  • The internal dashboard, showing the section 14 measures on one screen.
  • The integration service connecting messaging, records, dispatch and accounts.
  • Setup and configuration of the rented tools, plus the email and authentication settings in section 10.

What that is worth

  • Agencies typically quote £8,000 to £25,000 for a marketing site of this kind, and £15,000 to £40,000 for a client portal.
  • A migration of roughly 98 pages with a redirect map is usually a separate line item again.
  • Figures are indicative market ranges for context, not quotations.
  • The ongoing benefit is response time. Small fixes take hours rather than weeks.

Suggested order of work

Content fixes on the existing site come first, because they need no code. Then the domain decision and the page inventory. Then the website rebuild carrying the search pages across, followed by the quote form and the integrations. The corporate portal is the largest piece and sits after those. The dashboard follows once there is data worth showing.

Keeping it maintainable

Anything custom built needs someone to look after it. Four things reduce that risk: a content management system so routine changes need no developer, standard and widely used tooling rather than anything exotic, written handover documentation, and automatic deployment so releases do not depend on one person's laptop.

Compliance Checks

Four written answers worth obtaining. Each is cheap to check now and expensive to get wrong later.

AreaWhy it mattersSuggested action
Travel Selling a trip that combines two or more travel services for one price can make a business a package organiser under the 2018 travel regulations. Organisers are required to hold insolvency protection such as a bond, insurance or a trust account, and flights bring ATOL into it. Travel is now a core line, which makes this the most pressing of the four Written advice from a travel law adviser before travel is marketed further

Performance Measures

Seven lines, reviewed weekly. Without them the business is working on impressions.

  • Cash. Bank balance, thirteen week forecast, outstanding invoices.
  • Work coming in. Enquiries by channel, quotes sent, conversion rate, average job value.
  • Work delivered. Jobs completed, on time arrivals, complaints. Nothing unresolved beyond two days.
  • Referrals. Share of the month arriving by introduction, who sent it, whether they were thanked.
  • Reputation. New reviews this week, current rating, response time to enquiries.
  • Search. Visits from search, and which pages bring them.
  • Partners. Work received from partners, and fees earned on work referred out.
Twelve month targets

Proposed targets rather than forecasts. Accounts suitable to submit alongside a corporate tender. More than half of transport income from repeat or contracted work. Search traffic at or above where it stands today once the website has moved. Every client held on record, so that delivery does not depend on one individual being available.
